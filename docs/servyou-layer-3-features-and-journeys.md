# SERVYOU — LAYER 3: FEATURES AND JOURNEYS

This is the third layer of the Servyou platform definition, built section by section with the founder and locked before moving on. It defines what each user can actually do and the step-by-step journeys they take from arrival to outcome. Every feature here traces back to real evidence (gathered by researching Tunisie Freelance, Shopify, Converty, the Cash-on-Delivery reality, the dropshipping and winning-products culture, and the rise of UGC) or to a real user need and fear defined in Layer 2. No feature is included on imagination or imitation alone.

Layers 1 (Vision and Purpose) and 2 (Users and Roles) are complete and recorded separately. This document covers Layer 3 and includes the Pillar 4 Appendix A updates to the consumer, shop owner, and freelancer journeys — specifically the order status lifecycle (A.1), the cancellation discipline (A.2), and the buyer receipt confirmation (A.3).

## Guiding Design Principles for This Layer

Two principles, locked earlier, govern every feature below. First, design from evidence: each feature must answer "what real Tunisian behavior or need does this serve?" Second, progressive data collection: ask for the minimum at each stage and gather more only as the user goes deeper and a feature genuinely requires it.

## 3.1 — The Shared Foundation Journey (All Users)

Browsing is open to everyone without an account, so visitors arriving from a seller's social-media link land and see value immediately rather than hitting a wall. An account is required only when a user wants to act — request a product or service, post a job, or save a favorite.

Signup collects the minimum: email, password, full name, city or governorate, language preference (French or Arabic), and date of birth. Date of birth is required because of the age rule. The user confirms their email through the link Supabase Auth sends. Every new user begins as a consumer — the universal buying baseline.

Users can log in and out, stay logged in across visits, edit their profile, change their password, and reset a forgotten password, all handled by Supabase Auth. When a consumer chooses to become a seller, the upgrade flow checks that they are 18 or older (using the stored date of birth) before granting one seller capability — freelancer or shop owner. Under-18 users are refused the upgrade with a clear, kind message. Sellers keep their buying ability.

Progressive data collection applies throughout: shop details are requested when a user becomes a shop owner, freelancer details when they become a freelancer, professional credentials when they become a liberal professional in the future, and so on — never all at once, always at the moment the user's own needs require it.

## 3.2 — The Consumer Journey

The consumer arrives (often from a seller's social link), lands on a unified homepage mixing products and services, and browses freely without an account. They search by keyword and filter by category, city or governorate, and price range in Tunisian Dinars, which serves the consumer who has multiple needs over time.

They click into a product to see all photos, full description, price, stock status, and the shop's name and city; or into a service to see its description, starting price, delivery time, and the freelancer's name and headline; or into a full shop page or full freelancer profile. These complete, detailed pages directly serve the consumer's trust fear — the more real and complete a seller's page looks, the safer the consumer feels.

When the consumer acts, they click "Request to buy" (product) or "Request service" (freelancer), signing up or logging in at that moment if needed. For a product request, Servyou collects the consumer's delivery details — name, delivery address, and phone or WhatsApp number — because Cash on Delivery requires the seller to know where to deliver. The request becomes a `pending` order in the seller's dashboard, carrying those delivery details, and the consumer is shown a WhatsApp button with a pre-filled message naming the item, referencing Servyou, and openly assuming COD as the normal arrangement. Service requests use the simpler request-plus-WhatsApp flow without a delivery address; the resulting order also enters the seller's dashboard as `pending`.

**"My Requests" — order status transparency.** The consumer visits "My Requests" to track every product and service request, and the page now shows each order's position in the locked lifecycle from Pillar 4 §4.14.1: `pending` (the seller has not yet acknowledged), `accepted` (the seller committed to the order), `prepared` (the seller has packed or readied it — product orders only), `dispatched` (handed to delivery — product orders only), `in_delivery` (delivery in progress — product orders only), `arrived` (delivery has reached the buyer — product orders only), and `received` (the buyer confirmed receipt, which is the terminal success state). The consumer sees the same status the seller sees, at the same time. For service orders, the middle delivery states do not apply — service orders move directly from `accepted` to `received` once the buyer confirms the service was delivered, since there is nothing to dispatch and no physical delivery to track.

**Cancellation discipline.** The consumer can cancel a request, but the cancellation rules reflect the protect-both-sides discipline from Pillar 4 §4.14.2. Cancelling before dispatch (status is `pending`, `accepted`, or `prepared`) is free and immediate — the buyer changed their mind, the seller has not yet committed to delivery costs, no harm done. Cancelling after dispatch (status is `dispatched`, `in_delivery`, or `arrived`) requires a stated reason from the buyer, because the seller has incurred real costs by then, and the cancellation is logged on the buyer's own record. For service orders, the same logic applies but the dispatch line is conceptual rather than physical: cancelling before the freelancer accepts is free, cancelling after acceptance may require a reason at the freelancer's discretion. The UI presents these cancellation paths clearly: a "Cancel" button on pre-dispatch orders, a "Cancel with reason" prompt on post-dispatch orders, with the reason text saved alongside the order for dispute resolution if needed.

**Receipt confirmation.** When a product order reaches the `arrived` status, the consumer sees a clear "Confirm I received this" action on the My Requests page. Clicking it moves the order to `received` and records the timestamp on the order — the timestamp becomes the auditable evidence that the transaction completed successfully (from Pillar 4 §4.14.3). For service orders, the same confirmation action appears when the freelancer marks the service delivered (the order goes to status `arrived` for services too, used as a holding state before buyer confirmation). Without buyer confirmation, the order does not close — this is deliberate, because the confirmation is what gives the buyer real recourse and the seller real closure. If a buyer never confirms and the seller insists the order was completed, the dispute goes to the administrator with both sides' records visible.

The consumer can save favorites (a heart on any product or service) and view them on a "My Favorites" page. They can also post a job (see Section 3.5).

This product-request decision, combined with the lifecycle transparency, cancellation discipline, and receipt confirmation, directly serves both the consumer's COD comfort and the shop owner's need for organized orders. The order now holds personal delivery data and a possible cancellation reason, which Layer 5 must protect: visible only to the buyer and the specific seller, never public.

## 3.3 — The Shop Owner Journey

A consumer upgrades to shop owner (checked 18+) and creates their one shop: name, description, city or governorate, and optionally logo and banner. They add products through a form capturing title, description, price in TND, category, photos, and stock — with the form handling both stock-tracking sellers and dropshippers who do not hold inventory (for example, a toggle between "I track stock" and "always available / made to order"). They can edit and delete products and set each to active, hidden, or sold out.

**The Converty-style dashboard, now order-lifecycle-aware.** The shop owner gets a richer management dashboard: products organized by category, current stock levels, and units-sold counts, alongside the incoming orders list. Each order shows the product, the buyer's name and city, the delivery details the buyer provided for COD, the date, the current lifecycle status, and a WhatsApp button to contact the buyer. The order list is filterable by status so the seller can focus on the orders that need their attention now — typically the `pending` ones awaiting acceptance and the `accepted` or `prepared` ones awaiting their next action.

**Progressing an order through the lifecycle.** Each order shows the next action available to the seller, based on the locked lifecycle from Pillar 4 §4.14.1: from `pending`, the seller can `Accept` the order (commits to fulfilling it) or reject it by cancelling with a reason. From `accepted`, the seller marks the order `Prepared` once it is packed and ready. From `prepared`, they mark it `Dispatched` when they hand it to delivery — this is the dispatch line that triggers the cancellation-with-reason rule on the buyer side. From `dispatched`, they mark it `In Delivery` when delivery is actually in progress (this state may also be updated by the delivery integration in a future post-launch iteration). From `in_delivery`, they mark it `Arrived` when the delivery has reached the buyer. At this point, the seller waits — the order does not close to `received` until the buyer confirms receipt. The seller sees the order sitting in `arrived` status with a small "waiting for buyer confirmation" note. When the buyer confirms, the status moves to `received` and the order is closed from both sides. This is the shared truth that Pillar 4 §4.14.1 named: both buyer and seller see the same status at the same time, removing the anxiety of "where is my order?" and "did it actually arrive?"

**Seller cancellation.** The seller can also cancel an order at any pre-arrival stage — for instance, if the product is unexpectedly out of stock, if the buyer's delivery address turns out to be unreachable, or if the buyer becomes unresponsive after the initial request. Seller cancellation requires a reason like buyer cancellation does (the reason is logged on the order with `cancelled_by = 'seller'`), so the buyer can see why and so the platform has a record if patterns of unfair cancellation emerge.

**Cancellation history visibility.** The seller does not yet see the buyer's cancellation history at MVP — that visibility is part of the post-launch buyer-rating system sequenced in Pillar 4 §4.14.4. At MVP, the cancellation logs accumulate on the orders table, ready for the rating system to build on once there is enough data to make it meaningful.

Each shop has a public, shareable page showing the shop info and all active products — the link the owner places in their Instagram, Facebook, and TikTok bios, per the growth strategy. This richer dashboard is larger than a simple list and sizes the shop-owner build phase accordingly in Layer 7. The lifecycle-aware order management adds depth to the dashboard, not new pages — every order action lives inside the existing orders list, with the relevant button changing based on the order's current status.

## 3.4 — The Freelancer Journey

A consumer upgrades to freelancer (checked 18+) and builds their profile: photo, professional headline, bio, skills as tags, city or governorate, optional external portfolio link, and uploaded work samples shown in a clean gallery (images now, structured to allow short video later) on both the profile and service listings. The profile includes lightweight CV-like fields that are cheap and useful, such as years of experience, languages, and a short text list of past work or clients. Beyond these, the freelancer's configurable workspace (Pillar 4 §4.10) lets the freelancer optionally add working hours and availability, tools and software used, current workplace if employed elsewhere, education entries including current studies, certifications, and preferred payment method — each one optional, each one fillable when the freelancer is ready, never demanded all at once. This rich, complete profile serves both the freelancer's need to be taken seriously and the client's need to judge trustworthiness.

They add service listings through a form capturing title, description, starting price in TND (final price negotiated on WhatsApp), category, expected delivery time as flexible text, and optional sample images. They can edit and delete listings and set each to active or hidden.

**Service order lifecycle — the simpler flow.** The freelancer gets a dashboard listing incoming service requests newest-first — each showing the service, requester's name and city, date, current lifecycle status, and a WhatsApp button. Because service orders have no physical delivery, the lifecycle from Pillar 4 §4.14.1 is abbreviated for them: a service order moves from `pending` (newly requested) to `accepted` (the freelancer commits to the service) to `arrived` (the freelancer marks the service as delivered, which is the holding state awaiting buyer confirmation) to `received` (the buyer confirms they received the service, closing the order). The middle delivery states (`prepared`, `dispatched`, `in_delivery`) do not apply to service orders and are skipped — the UI does not show those buttons or labels for service orders, only the states that apply.

**Seller-side actions on service orders.** From `pending`, the freelancer either accepts (status becomes `accepted`) or cancels with a reason. From `accepted`, the freelancer works on and delivers the service; when delivery is complete, they mark it `Delivered` (status becomes `arrived`). The buyer then has the opportunity to confirm receipt of the service — when they confirm, the order moves to `received` and closes. The same dispute-resolution discipline applies as for product orders: the `received_at` timestamp is the evidence that the service was confirmed received. The freelancer cannot mark an order `received` unilaterally; that confirmation belongs to the buyer.

**Cancellation for services.** The same protect-both-sides discipline applies: a buyer cancelling a service order before the freelancer has accepted is free; cancelling after acceptance is logged on the buyer's record with a reason. A freelancer cancelling a service order they had accepted is logged on their own record with `cancelled_by = 'seller'` and a reason. These records form the foundation for the post-launch buyer-and-freelancer rating systems.

Their public freelancer profile has a shareable link for their social bios. They can also browse the job board and respond to job posts (see Section 3.5).

**Profile design philosophy (locked).** The freelancer profile is inspired by two references without copying either: LinkedIn, as the benchmark for professional credibility and trust; and Instagram, as the benchmark for ease of use, clarity, and visual friendliness. Servyou takes the spirit of professionalism from one and the spirit of simplicity from the other, expressed in its own genuinely Tunisian way. Inspiration, never imitation.

**Explicitly not in the MVP (deferred):** LinkedIn-style connections, endorsements, or recommendations; a full structured work-history and education system (the optional configurable fields from Pillar 4 §4.10 are the lightweight MVP version; deeper structured work-history is post-MVP); any social feed or following mechanism; complex profile customization. Uploaded media and the richer profile will size the freelancer build phase larger in Layer 7.

## 3.5 — The Job-Posting System

This reverse flow lets a consumer describe a need and have freelancers come to them. It is strongly evidence-backed, dominating the Tunisian freelance market.

From the consumer's side: a logged-in consumer creates a job post with a title, detailed description, budget in TND (fixed or range), category, city or governorate (or remote), desired deadline, and required skills. The post publishes to the job board. In a "My Job Posts" area the consumer sees which freelancers responded and can mark the post filled (closing it), edit it, or delete it.

From the freelancer's side: a freelancer browses the job board, filters by category, city, budget, and skills, clicks into a post to read it fully, and responds via a WhatsApp button with a pre-filled message identifying the job and referencing Servyou. Revealing contact at the moment of response is acceptable because the consumer publicly posted asking for service.

Job posts carry a status: open (on the board), filled (closed, kept for both parties' records), expired (auto-closed after a set time such as 30 days), or deleted.

**Response limits (locked).** To protect quality and fairness without any paid Units economy, two light, free limits apply. A post caps at a reasonable number of responses (starting around 10), after which it stops accepting more — protecting the consumer from flooding and ensuring responders are actually seen. Each freelancer can hold only a limited number of active responses at once (starting around 5) — preventing any one freelancer from monopolizing opportunities and leaving room for new and quieter freelancers. The numbers are tunable. This directly serves the Layer 1 value of not favoring the big over the small, and the founder's goal that everyone gets a chance to work.

## 3.6 — The Administrator Journey

The admin logs into a separate, protected dashboard (access granted deliberately, not by public signup). They can see all users with role, city, join date, activity, and status, and suspend a rule-breaker (blocks login, preserves data) or remove a genuinely bad actor. They can see all content — shops, products, freelancer profiles, services, job posts — and hide or remove anything fake, offensive, or rule-breaking. They handle reports users submit via a report button on content, reviewing and acting on each. They resolve disputes between users. They grant admin access to other trusted team members.

The admin dashboard cannot touch raw data or code; it acts only through these safe, limited tools, per the locked data-protection separation between the moderator role and the developer/owner role.

**Statistics (locked).** The dashboard includes a lightweight statistics view showing aggregate, anonymous numbers — totals of users, shops, freelancers, products, services, job posts, and especially completed orders (such as "completed orders this month"). These serve three aligned purposes: confirming Servyou is working (Layer 1 success measures), supporting moderation, and providing shareable social proof for marketing and growth. Strict boundary: only aggregate, anonymous statistics may be viewed and shared publicly; individual personal data (who bought what from whom, a person's history or activity) is never shared publicly and is accessed only when genuinely necessary for moderation. Aggregate numbers for marketing; individual data for careful moderation only.

**Dispute resolution context.** With the order status lifecycle and cancellation discipline locked from Pillar 4, the admin's dispute resolution role gains structured evidence to work with. When a buyer reports an issue with an order, the admin can see the full lifecycle history of that order (which status transitions happened and when), the `received_at` timestamp if the buyer confirmed receipt, and any cancellation reason if cancellation was involved. This gives the admin a neutral starting ground for dispute resolution instead of pure he-said-she-said, serving both the honesty value and the protect-users value.

## 3.7 — Cross-Cutting Features

**Homepage (locked).** A richer, action-driving homepage whose purpose is to push the visitor to act: a prominent search bar (captures intent), category cards such as Beauté, Santé, Développement, Montage Vidéo, UGC, and Marketing (capture interest), a trending or "winning products" section (creates urgency, playing to Tunisian product-hunting culture), featured or top shops and freelancers (social proof easing the trust fear, and aspiration for sellers), and a recent or fresh items mix (keeps the platform feeling alive). The trending rule starts as admin-featured at launch, when data is sparse and full control is valuable, and shifts toward automatic "most-requested" as real activity grows; the exact rule is settled in Layer 4.

**Other cross-cutting features (locked).** Site-wide search across products, services, shops, and freelancers; category pages mixing products and services, refinable by city and price; city and governorate pages; mobile-first design throughout, because most Tunisians browse on phones; and the full interface in French (default) and Arabic, with right-to-left layout for Arabic and an always-visible language switcher. English is not in the MVP.

## Bridge to Later Layers

Every feature here feeds the data model (Layer 4), the security model (Layer 5), and the build plan (Layer 7). Product orders carry personal delivery data that must be protected, and now also a possible cancellation reason text that is similarly sensitive. The freelancer profile and shop dashboard require media storage and richer structures, including the new optional configurable fields and child tables from Layer 4 reflecting Pillar 4 §4.10. The job-posting system needs tables for posts and responses with status and the two response limits. The homepage trending section needs a defined rule. The two-language interface shapes how every screen and string is built. The order lifecycle from Pillar 4 §4.14 — status transparency, cancellation discipline, receipt confirmation — adds depth to the existing seller dashboards and the buyer's My Requests page rather than creating new top-level surfaces, with the data model already designed to carry it (Layer 4 §4.6). When the schema and build plan are designed, each table, rule, and phase must trace back to a feature locked here.
