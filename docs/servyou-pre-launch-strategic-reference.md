# SERVYOU PRE-LAUNCH STRATEGIC REFERENCE — STANDARDS FROM GLOBAL LEADERS

This document exists to do one thing: bring the accumulated wisdom of how successful marketplaces actually work into Servyou's pre-launch planning, in a form Moatez can return to whenever a decision needs framing. It is a companion to the seven foundation documents already in Project Knowledge (`project-overview.md`, `platform-pages-and-system.md`, `product.md`, `data-model.md`, `architecture.md`, `engineering-standards.md`, `roadmap.md`), and it is written in the same prose-first style so it slots in cleanly.

The brief Moatez gave was specific. Five things needed real research, not pattern-matching from memory: how global marketplace leaders build trust and credibility from day one, how the pages and identity should come together (especially the landing page and hero section as marketing surfaces), how the backend and frontend relationship should be kept safe from leaks and silent failures, how a user should see the latest numbers in their dashboard without mistakes, and how a solo beginning developer can admin and moderate a real platform without going under. This document covers all five, plus the connective tissue that turns them into a single coherent pre-launch plan.

It draws on contemporary marketplace, frontend, security, and platform-operations research from 2025–2026, including post-Jumia-Tunisia analysis, the cold-start literature from Andrew Chen and others, Tunisian e-commerce reality (Mallatech, Converty, the surviving classified players), the modern Supabase + Next.js stack guidance, MENA-specific UX research, and the small-team monitoring stack as it stands today. Where contemporary practice has shifted from older advice, the newer view is the one taken.

What this document is not is a design system. The design system is its own multi-week piece of work, sequenced after the basics are done per `roadmap.md`. This document is the strategic and engineering reference that informs the design system when its time comes.

## 1. The two-sided marketplace problem and how the leaders solved it

Servyou is a multi-sided platform. Consumers, freelancers, shop owners, and an internal administrator role all need to coexist on the same surface, and value flows only when buyers find sellers worth buying from and sellers find buyers worth serving. This is the structural condition that defines marketplaces and that has produced the largest collection of failed startups in the technology industry. The literature on why marketplaces fail is rich, and the patterns are remarkably consistent.

The fundamental problem is the cold-start problem. When the platform has zero users, demand will not arrive because there is no supply worth searching, and supply will not arrive because there is no demand worth supplying. Founders who try to solve both sides at once burn their resources without ever crossing the critical-mass threshold where either side becomes self-sustaining. Andrew Chen's *The Cold Start Problem* and the contemporary platforms it studies all converge on the same answer: solve supply first, and do it manually before any automation ships.

Uber recruited drivers before they had riders, often paying them through subsidized hours just to keep them on the road. Airbnb sent a photography team to each new city to take professional photos of host properties one at a time, because beautiful listings were the inventory the demand side would arrive for. Etsy's founders went to craft fairs in person and recruited individual artisan sellers one conversation at a time. Lyft started with W-2 drivers who could be told where to be, when. Each one of these companies spent its first six to eighteen months doing things that did not scale — and that founder labor, expensive on paper, was the only thing that bought them survival to phase two.

The 2026 evolution of this playbook adds one important refinement: even when AI tools can now generate synthetic supply or scrape competing platforms at very low cost, the cold-start work still needs to happen because the marketplace's matching layer cannot be trained on fake data. The 2026 best-practice gate is roughly one hundred successful pair-completions of real supply and real demand before any algorithmic matching turns on, with the founder sampling around ten percent of automated matches weekly for six months after that. Marketplaces that ship with no concierge phase report twice the supply-side churn at month three.

For Servyou specifically, this maps directly onto the existing strategy in `product.md`: the social media layer (Instagram, Facebook, TikTok) is the marketing engine, and the platform is the transaction and organization hub. Shop owners and freelancers bring their existing audiences with them. That is the supply-first move done right — Moatez does not need to acquire supply from cold; the supply arrives via the social media bios of sellers who already have followings. The platform's job is to be the destination that those followings can land on and trust. This is a structurally easier cold-start than Airbnb or Uber faced because the supply-side acquisition cost is essentially zero per seller (their existing social media presence brings them).

The first hundred sellers Servyou onboards in the first month will be Moatez's most important assets. They should be hand-recruited, briefed personally on how the platform works, and given direct access to Moatez through WhatsApp for the first few weeks. The temptation to automate this will be strong; it should be resisted. The intelligence Moatez gets from these conversations — what shop owners actually need from a dashboard, what freelancers worry about, what makes a buyer feel safe paying COD to an unknown shop — is the substrate the rest of the platform's evolution will be built on.

Geographic discipline matters. The 2026 marketplace playbook is uncompromising about this: density beats breadth. Launch narrow, then expand — density beats breadth every time. For Servyou this means launching one governorate, not all twenty-four. Tunis-Ariana-Ben Arous is the natural cluster: 35% of Tunisia's population, the highest internet penetration, the highest concentration of dropshippers and freelancers already active on social commerce. If the first thousand active users are clustered geographically, the network effect activates faster — buyers find more sellers nearby, delivery is cheaper, word of mouth spreads through real social networks instead of being diluted across the country.

Liquidity is the metric that matters at this stage, not signups. Liquidity in a marketplace is the probability that a buyer arriving will find what they want and complete a transaction within a reasonable session. A platform with one thousand signups and ten transactions per week has worse liquidity than a platform with one hundred signups and twenty transactions per week, even though the first looks bigger. The 2026 GTM literature is consistent about this: track liquidity, not vanity volume. For Servyou, this translates to tracking activated buyers (those who completed at least one COD request) and active sellers (those who received at least one request in the trailing fourteen days), not the count of total profiles created.

## 2. The landing page that converts visitors into users

The landing page is the single most consequential surface in marketing terms. It is where social media traffic, organic search traffic, paid traffic if and when Servyou starts spending on it, and word-of-mouth referrals all converge. A landing page that converts thirty percent of arrivals into signups is worth roughly six times a landing page that converts five percent, and the cost of building each is the same. This makes the landing page the highest-leverage piece of pre-launch work after the engine itself, and it deserves more serious thought than founders usually give it.

The contemporary research on landing page conversion is dense and consistent. Visitors decide whether to stay or leave within roughly three seconds of arrival, and first impressions form in about fifty milliseconds. This means the visible top of the page — the hero section in modern terminology — is doing the bulk of the conversion work. Everything below the fold is for visitors who have already decided not to bounce; the work of converting the bouncers happens above it.

The 2026 standard for hero sections has moved away from static taglines and toward story-driven visual demonstrations. Leading examples like Notion, Linear, and Framer use micro-animations and product workflow illustrations to show transformation within the first critical seconds of engagement. The pattern that emerges from the highest-converting B2B and consumer marketplaces is a hero that contains five elements in this order of visual prominence: a headline that names the outcome the visitor wants, a subheadline that explains how the platform delivers that outcome, a primary visual that demonstrates the product in use, a single primary call-to-action that names the next step, and a slim band of trust indicators (user count, logos, ratings) that establish credibility.

Headlines that convert are short. The average high-performing H1 headline contains under 8 words (44 characters maximum). This constraint is not arbitrary; it forces the founder to articulate the platform's value in language a tired thumb-scrolling visitor will process without effort. Jargon, brand-internal terminology, and meta-language about the platform itself all need to leave the headline. The headline names what the visitor gets, in their own words.

For Servyou, the headline contenders Moatez should consider all share a structure: a noun phrase that names the destination ("a home", "a place", "a workspace"), a qualifier that locates it specifically in Tunisia, and a hint at the multi-sidedness without overwhelming detail. Strong candidates in French: *"Le marché tunisien qui fonctionne enfin"* (eight words, names the outcome, names the place). *"Vendez, achetez, travaillez — en Tunisien"* (six words plus the parenthetical, names the three core actions, claims Tunisian identity). *"Une vraie maison pour l'économie tunisienne"* (six words, evokes the founding metaphor in `product.md`). The strongest of these is the one that survives the test of being read aloud to five Tunisian friends without explanation; whichever one earns nods first is the one that lives in the hero.

The subheadline does the work the headline cannot. It expands the promise into one sentence that names the specific benefits. For Servyou this is where the Tunisian-not-foreign positioning gets articulated — payment in dinars, delivery in governorates, Cash on Delivery as the normal arrangement, French and Arabic at the user's choice. One sentence, around twenty to thirty words, written for a thirty-year-old Tunisian shop owner or a twenty-two-year-old Tunisian freelancer scrolling on their phone at midnight.

The primary CTA is one button, one verb, one outcome. Landing pages should have a single, consistent CTA repeated strategically rather than the four-button menu of "Start Free Trial / Watch Demo / Download Guide / Contact Sales" that diffuses visitor attention. For Servyou, the primary CTA is *Créer un compte gratuit* or *Commencer*, and it appears prominently in the hero, mid-page after the value proposition is built up, and at the closing of the page. Every visit to the page should make the next action obvious.

Trust indicators in the hero band require care. For a brand-new platform there are no logos of marquee customers, no five-figure user counts to display, no review averages with three significant figures behind them. The trust indicators Servyou can honestly show on day one are different: a count of Tunisian governorates served (24), a count of active categories (the 14 from the categories table), a clear statement that the platform charges no commission on transactions at launch, and the privacy-first commitment that data stays in the EU's protected zone (Frankfurt). These honest day-one signals serve the visitor's need for "is this real" without manufacturing false credibility.

Below the hero, the next section structures the value proposition into three benefit blocks. Airbnb effectively communicates its unique selling points, such as "Unique stays," "Local experiences," and "Book with confidence" — three benefits, each carrying a one-word claim and an icon. For Servyou, the three benefits are easy to name from the existing product positioning: *Tunisien d'abord* (Tunisian first — dinars, governorates, COD), *Honnête sur les prix* (honest pricing — no hidden fees, what you see is what you pay), *Vos données, votre maison* (your data, your home — privacy posture, no foreign data exploitation).

Further down, the page narrates the three core user journeys, one section per journey. The shop owner journey: post a shop, list products, share the link on Instagram, receive COD requests, manage them through the lifecycle dashboard. The freelancer journey: build a profile, list services, browse the job board, respond via WhatsApp. The consumer journey: browse without an account, find a shop or freelancer, request via WhatsApp, track the order in *Mes demandes*. Each journey gets a two-sentence description and a sample screenshot of the relevant product surface. This is the "show, don't tell" half of the page.

After the journeys, the FAQ section addresses the questions a hesitant visitor will ask. Five to seven questions, each answered in one or two sentences. The most important questions, drawn directly from Pillar 4's user fears: *Comment est-ce que Servyou empêche les arnaques?* (How does Servyou prevent scams?), *Et si le produit livré n'est pas ce que j'ai commandé?* (And if the delivered product isn't what I ordered?), *Servyou prend-il une commission?* (Does Servyou take a commission?), *Mes données sont-elles vendues à des tiers?* (Is my data sold to third parties?). Honest answers; if the answer is *not yet but soon*, say so. The Tunisian audience appreciates straight talk more than marketing language.

The closing section restates the primary CTA and adds one final emotional anchor — a single line that captures why Servyou exists, written in the founder's voice. Something like *"Tunis mérite une vraie place du marché. Nous la construisons. Rejoignez-nous."* This is the line that gets remembered.

The footer is its own infrastructure, covered in chapter four.

One critical conversion detail: removing the navigation menu from B2B landing pages significantly improves conversion rates by eliminating distractions. For Servyou, this means that *paid traffic landing pages* (when paid acquisition starts post-launch) should have no top navigation — only the logo, no menu links, no language toggle even. The home page that organic visitors arrive at can keep its navigation because organic visitors arrive with curiosity and want to explore; paid visitors arrive ready to convert and any sideways link is an exit.

## 3. The hero section deep dive

The hero deserves its own chapter because every other section of the landing page exists to support it, and because the difference between a hero that works and a hero that wastes the traffic above it is measured in factors, not percentages.

The pattern that has emerged across the highest-converting marketplaces in 2026 is *story-driven visual demonstration*. The 2026 design standard shifts from static taglines to story-driven hero sections that visually demonstrate product value within 3-5 seconds. What this means in practice is that the hero visual is not a stock photo of happy people, not an abstract illustration, and not a static product screenshot. It is a sequence — either an actual short video loop, or an animated interface mockup, or a series of stills that build a small narrative. The narrative shows transformation: the world before the platform, the moment of using the platform, and the world after.

For Servyou's hero, the transformation that needs to be shown is the one from *scattered, anxious, foreign-tool-driven Tunisian commerce* to *organized, trusted, Tunisian-native commerce*. Concrete visualization possibilities, ranked roughly by feasibility for a solo founder building this in 2026: an animation that starts with a phone screen showing four messaging app icons (Instagram, Facebook, WhatsApp, TikTok) all blinking with order notifications, then collapses them into one Servyou dashboard with all those orders organized into the eight-stage lifecycle; a stylized scene of a shopkeeper writing orders by hand in a notebook, dissolving into the same shop owner using the Servyou orders dashboard; a side-by-side mockup of the consumer's "My Requests" view showing four orders moving through their lifecycle stages with timestamps. Any of these requires illustration work that does not yet exist; the hero is therefore a multi-week piece of design work in its own right, and the launching version may need to be a still that gets replaced once the animated version is ready.

The hero's responsive behavior is important. Mobile is where most Tunisian traffic will arrive, per the existing `product.md` analysis. The mobile hero is not the desktop hero shrunk; it is a deliberate single-column layout that puts the headline at the top, the primary CTA immediately below in a way that fits within the first phone-screen height, and the supporting visual below that. The desktop hero can use a two-column split (text left, visual right, in LTR mode; reversed for RTL Arabic) because there is room to do so, but the mobile hero cannot.

Typography in the hero earns special attention. The headline weight in the hero should be one step heavier than the rest of the page — if the page uses a 600 font weight for headings, the hero headline can be 700 or 800. This is not because heavier is intrinsically better, but because the hero needs to win the eye against everything else competing for it on the visitor's screen. The headline size scales with viewport: roughly 32-40px on mobile, 56-72px on desktop, never smaller than that on either.

Color in the hero pulls attention toward the CTA, not the visual. The visual is interesting; the CTA is *actionable*. The CTA's background color should be the highest-contrast color on the page (not necessarily the most saturated; contrast against the surrounding context is what matters). Tunisian color associations are covered in chapter six, but for hero CTA purposes, the practical rule is: pick one signature color, make the CTA that color, and use it nowhere else in the hero. A red CTA on a blue-and-white hero will win the eye; a red CTA on a hero already full of red highlights will lose it.

The headline copywriting deserves a final note. The pattern that has earned the highest conversion rates across 2025-2026 in B2B and consumer studies is *outcome-focused, not feature-focused*. Pages that demonstrate transformation rather than listing capabilities consistently outperform traditional approaches. A feature-focused Servyou headline says "Marketplace tunisien avec paiement à la livraison" (Tunisian marketplace with cash on delivery). An outcome-focused Servyou headline says "Vendre et acheter, sans peur, en Tunisien" (Sell and buy, without fear, in Tunisian). The first describes what Servyou is; the second describes what the visitor gets. The second wins on every conversion test the literature has run.

## 4. The footer and standalone pages every platform needs

The standalone pages — Terms of Service, Privacy Policy, Cookie Policy, Accessibility Statement, About, Contact, FAQ, 404, 500 — are the unglamorous infrastructure that makes a platform feel real to a careful visitor and that keeps the platform legally defensible to a regulator or a payment processor doing due diligence. They are not optional, and they are not the kind of work that can be left until launch week.

The legal pages need a lawyer review before publication. Trust is the lifeline of online marketplaces. By prioritising transparent transactions, user reviews, customer support, and identity verification, platforms can foster a sense of security and reliability among their users. For Tunisia specifically, the relevant statutes are the Loi organique n° 2004-63 relative à la protection des données à caractère personnel (data protection), Article 25-bis of the Loi sur le commerce électronique (e-commerce regulation), and the labor and tax frameworks that touch any platform that mediates economic activity between parties. Moatez does not need to be a lawyer; he needs a one-hour consultation with a Tunisian lawyer who specializes in digital economy work, equipped with a draft of each legal document that the lawyer reviews and amends rather than writing from scratch. The cost of this is real but small (typically 500-1500 TND for the consultation plus revisions, depending on the lawyer's reputation) and it is the single best insurance Servyou buys at this stage.

The Terms of Service should be honest and short. The pattern that builds trust here, paradoxically, is *limiting* the platform's claims of liability transfer to the user. A Terms of Service that says "We disclaim all warranties, you accept all risk, we are not responsible for anything" reads as a foreign template applied unedited. A Terms of Service that says "Voici ce que nous faisons, voici ce que vous faites, voici ce qui se passe en cas de problème" — in plain language with section headings a non-lawyer can navigate — is the kind that earns the reader's trust by being readable.

The Privacy Policy is where Servyou's strongest selling point gets articulated: data stays in the EU's protected zone, Servyou collects only what it needs, third-party trackers are not embedded, and the user has the right to access and deletion. This is genuine differentiation against the older foreign-template platforms that operated in Tunisia, and the Privacy Policy is the place to say so. Every section should be specific to Servyou's actual data practices, not copied from a Generator That Outputs A Privacy Policy For You. The lawyer review especially matters here.

The Cookie Policy and Cookie Consent Banner are paired. The banner appears on first visit with three buttons: Accept All, Reject All, and Customize. The cookies Servyou actually sets at launch are minimal — the Supabase Auth session cookie, possibly Vercel analytics anonymized counters. The consent banner should be honest about how little is actually set, because most Tunisian users expect to see four or five tracking categories and a "Reject All" that secretly keeps tracking running. Servyou's banner being honest about a small footprint is itself a trust signal.

The Accessibility Statement declares Servyou's commitment to WCAG 2.2 Level AA. It names what has been done (keyboard navigation across all critical flows, color contrast ratios verified, touch targets ≥44×44 pixels, screen reader labels), acknowledges what hasn't been done yet (full screen reader testing in Arabic mode, alt text on user-uploaded images), and provides a contact method for users who hit accessibility barriers. The statement is short — one page — but it must be specific to Servyou's actual posture, not a copied template.

The About page is the founder's personal voice on the platform. Three to five paragraphs covering: who built Servyou (one founder, learning to code as he built it, with the AI-assisted-development reality acknowledged honestly), why it was built (the Tunisian gap, the foreign-platform exit, the founding insight), and what the values are (the three core values from `product.md`: protect users, honest about money, genuinely Tunisian). A photograph of Moatez is useful but not required if he prefers not to be public-facing. The About page is where emotional connection happens, and emotional connection is what turns visitors into evangelists.

The Contact page lists: a primary email address that is actually monitored (something like `bonjour@servyou.tn` reads warmer than `contact@servyou.tn`), the expected response time (set this honestly: 24-48 hours during the first three months while Moatez is responding personally), an optional contact form for visitors who prefer not to email, links to whatever social media accounts Servyou maintains, and the legal entity name and address required by Tunisian commerce regulation.

The FAQ page answers the questions that Pillar 4's user research already surfaced: questions about scams, about COD safety, about who can sell, about how disputes are resolved, about the platform's fees (none at launch), about data privacy, about why French and Arabic but not English. Each answer is a single paragraph. The FAQ uses an accordion pattern so visitors can scan the questions and only expand the ones relevant to them.

The 404 page is critical and usually neglected. The default Next.js 404 page is functional but cold; a custom 404 that says something warm in French and offers a search bar plus links to the main categories converts a dead-end visit into a continuing session about ten percent of the time. The 500 page similarly should acknowledge that something went wrong, apologize briefly, and offer either a retry button or a path back to a known-working page. Both pages should match the platform's visual style, not look like raw Tailwind defaults.

The footer that appears on every page contains: the Servyou logo and a one-line tagline; the secondary navigation links (About, Contact, FAQ); the legal links (Terms, Privacy, Cookies, Accessibility); the language toggle for visitors who arrived on the wrong language; the social media links to whatever accounts exist (Instagram and Facebook are the bare minimum for a Tunisian platform); the copyright notice with the current year; and the legal entity information. The footer reads as quiet, organized, professional — the visual opposite of the busy crowded footers that older platforms put together. Generous whitespace, small text, neutral colors, no shouting.

One specific detail that improves credibility for Tunisian users: include a phone number or at minimum a WhatsApp link in the footer or contact page. Arabic users often value strong trust indicators such as ... Including messaging apps such as WhatsApp can also improve engagement. Tunisian users in particular expect to be able to reach a real human if something goes wrong, and a platform that hides behind a contact form alone loses a category of trust that competing platforms (and their WhatsApp-based replacements) hold.

## 5. Building trust signals for a brand-new platform

Trust is the substance Servyou is selling. Buyers worry about being scammed by sellers they have never met; sellers worry about non-paying or vanishing buyers; both worry about the platform itself being a fly-by-night that takes their data and disappears. The trust problem is most acute in the first ninety days because every signal that established platforms use — review counts, transaction volumes, partner logos, press mentions — does not yet exist for Servyou. The early platform must build trust through other means.

The good news from the research is that the catalog of effective trust signals for early-stage platforms is well-mapped. The signals that work fall into three categories: signals of identity, signals of behavior, and signals of construction.

Signals of identity are the platform's claims about who runs it and where it lives. A real founder name, a real face on the About page, a real Tunisian address in the footer, a working email that gets responded to — these establish that Servyou is operated by an identifiable Tunisian rather than an anonymous offshore entity. This sounds obvious but it is where most failed marketplaces collapsed in trust terms; visitors who could not find a real human accountable for the platform left and did not come back.

Signals of behavior are how the platform actually acts toward early users. 60% of consumers expect responses within 10 minutes, with 90% rating immediate response as important. For the first ninety days, Moatez personally responding to every email and WhatsApp message within an hour is a behavior signal that no marketing claim can match. The Tunisian user who emails Servyou at 2am with a confused question about how the order lifecycle works and gets a real reply at 8am from Moatez is the user who tells their friends about Servyou. That word-of-mouth, in the early ninety days, is worth more than any paid acquisition would buy. The discipline is to maintain this responsiveness even as it gets harder; the moment Servyou stops responding personally is the moment it becomes another platform.

Signals of construction are the visible quality of the product itself. A platform that loads fast, never shows broken images, never throws JavaScript errors, never returns blank pages, and behaves consistently across mobile and desktop carries a non-verbal trust signal that says "people who know what they are doing built this." Conversely, a platform that takes seven seconds to load on a Tunis 4G connection, that occasionally returns a blank product page when the buyer clicks on a listing, that has small French typos visible in the UI, telegraphs the opposite. Quality of construction is a trust signal even when no one consciously names it as such.

The research on trust signals has matured to the point where it can distinguish what helps from what does not. Despite the signal's theoretical credibility, this study finds no significant effects on seller pricing or perceived trustworthiness. Results indicate that signal effectiveness might diminish when platforms already feature numerous strong cues (e.g. star ratings, badges). This suggests a potential saturation point in digital trust signaling. This means that piling badge upon badge ("Verified Tunisian Business", "Trusted Seller Since 2026", "Secure Platform", "PCI Compliant") at the bottom of every page does not multiply trust — past a certain point, additional badges produce diminishing returns and can even feel performative. The pre-launch trust strategy for Servyou should focus on getting *two or three* trust signals right rather than producing fifteen weak ones.

The two or three for Servyou are: the order lifecycle transparency (buyer and seller both see the same eight-stage status, which is an unusual level of openness for a Tunisian COD platform and worth showing prominently), the cancellation logging discipline (buyers know that their cancellation reasons are recorded and become part of their reputation, which discourages frivolous cancellations and makes the platform feel like it has real accountability mechanisms), and the Tunisian-data-residency commitment (the data lives in Frankfurt under EU protection, not in some unspecified American cloud, and Servyou is explicit about this in the Privacy Policy and About page).

Mixed ratings are more trustworthy than perfect ratings, when reviews eventually arrive post-launch. Mixed ratings (4.2-4.7 stars) actually convert better than perfect 5.0 ratings by appearing more authentic. This means that when the rating system launches (deferred to post-MVP per `roadmap.md`), Servyou should not panic when sellers do not have perfect 5.0 averages. Sellers with 4.3 averages that include a few honest 3-star reviews will outconvert sellers with manufactured 5.0 averages, because buyers have learned to discount perfect ratings as fake. This is counterintuitive to founders who instinctively want every signal to be perfect, and worth holding onto when the temptation to inflate ratings arises.

The review volume threshold matters more than the rating. Products need a minimum of 10 reviews to achieve meaningful conversion impact, with optimal performance at 50-100 reviews. ... Recent reviews (within 3 months) carry 3x more weight. This means that the first ten reviews a shop or freelancer earns are roughly worth the next forty in conversion impact, and old reviews fade in influence faster than founders expect. Operationally, this argues for an active program of asking buyers to leave reviews after completed transactions, in the first ninety days, even if (especially if) the request feels uncomfortable. The discomfort fades after the first ten asks; the trust the early reviews build does not.

Behaviorally, scarcity signals work but only when they are real. Live inventory counts work because they are verifiable. ... Showing inventory when stock was below 10 units increased immediate purchases by 28%. The Servyou product data model already supports this (the `stock_count` column on `products` when `tracks_stock=true`), and the product detail page should display "Plus que 3 en stock" when stock is low. This is automatic, costs nothing to implement, and converts. Critically, it should never be shown when stock is high; *"847 en stock"* removes urgency and harms conversion. The conditional rendering should be: show stock count only when below ten units.

## 6. Brand identity and design foundations for Tunisian platforms

The full design system is a deferred multi-week piece of work per `roadmap.md`. What this chapter covers is the strategic frame that the design system will be built into when its time arrives. Decisions made here should hold across whatever specific tokens and components emerge from the actual design work.

Tunisia is in the MENA region linguistically and culturally, with its own North African distinctness. The color, typography, and interaction conventions that established platforms have converged on for Arabic-language users are a useful starting baseline, but Tunisia's specific cultural posture — French-Arabic bilingual, Mediterranean, more secular than Gulf MENA in everyday commerce — means some adjustments matter.

Color associations carry weight in MENA contexts in ways that matter for marketplace trust, and the design system work — when its time arrives — will need to decide on a palette with awareness of these associations. The strategic frame is what this chapter captures, not the specific color picks; those belong to the brand-identity work Moatez owns.

Typography in a bilingual French-Arabic interface has specific constraints worth naming at the framework level, even though the actual font picks belong to the brand-identity work Moatez owns. The constraint that matters most: the French font and the Arabic font chosen need to share visual weight, proportion, and feeling. Mismatched pairings — a heavy serif French face next to a delicate naskh Arabic face, for instance — break the platform's visual coherence at every language switch. The specific pairing is its own decision when the brand work begins; the requirement that it be a deliberate pairing rather than a defaulted one is the framework point.

A practical detail in Arabic typography that matters at the framework level regardless of which fonts get chosen: Arabic text takes approximately twenty percent more horizontal space than the equivalent French text. This means that buttons designed for French labels can break in Arabic if they are sized tightly; the design system should set buttons to size to their content with a minimum width that accommodates the longer Arabic translation.

RTL layout is not just text direction flipping. Arabic users often scan content, compare options, and move through pages within a right-to-left mental model. That influences navigation, content order, CTA placement, and even where reassurance messages should appear. What this means in practice for Servyou: when the language is Arabic, navigation menus open from the right, primary CTAs sit to the right of secondary actions (mirroring the left-side default in French), and the visual reading order of cards and listings starts from the top-right rather than the top-left. Tailwind's logical properties (`ps-4` instead of `pl-4`, `start-0` instead of `left-0`) handle most of this automatically when the `dir="rtl"` attribute is set on the HTML element, but layout testing in actual Arabic mode is necessary to catch the cases where the logical properties don't quite get it right.

Mobile primacy is the operative assumption. Tunisian users overwhelmingly browse on phones, with 4G connections that are reliable in cities and patchier outside them. The design system needs to be designed for the mobile single-column flow first, with desktop being the elaborated case. For mobile design, remember that smartphones dominate Middle Eastern internet use. Put your primary actions where right-handed users naturally reach—the right side of the screen. The reachability constraint matters: the bottom-right of the screen (for right-handed thumb operation) is where the primary action goes; the top of the screen is where the visual hierarchy starts but not where actions live.

Imagery in the design system needs Tunisian-specific consideration. Stock photos of generic young people drinking coffee read as imported; they signal foreign-platform rather than Tunisian-platform. The imagery that earns Tunisian trust is local: Tunisian scenes, Tunisian people doing Tunisian things, Tunisian street signs visible in product photography backgrounds. This does not mean every image must be stereotypical (the Sidi Bou Said blue and white, the camels, the carpet markets); it means the imagery should be unmistakably Tunisian without being touristy. Sourcing this imagery is itself a project — it may justify hiring a Tunisian photographer for a week to produce a library of platform imagery, which would itself be a marketing event.

The brand voice for Servyou's copy should match the founder's actual voice. The temptation when writing platform copy is to inflate the language into marketing-speak; resist this. The voice that has worked for `product.md` and the rest of the foundation documents — direct, warm, honest about what is and is not delivered, slightly slower than corporate writing — is the same voice that should appear in the platform's UI labels, error messages, empty states, and notification text. Consistency of voice across product surfaces is itself a brand signal.

The visual identity needs a logo. A solo founder cannot do this with full justice; it is worth either hiring a Tunisian designer for a week-long engagement to produce a logo and three or four core brand assets, or using a high-quality generated logo service (the better ones produce results that are not embarrassing) and iterating on it. The logo should be readable at small sizes (favicon, mobile header), should work in both monochrome and color, and should not depend on stylized text in a font that might not render. A wordmark logo is usually easier than a symbol-and-wordmark logo for an early-stage platform. The specific direction the logo takes belongs to the brand-identity work Moatez owns; the framework here is the set of constraints any acceptable logo must satisfy.

## 7. Backend-frontend reliability — the safe boundary

Servyou's architecture is already strong on this dimension. The Row Level Security posture, the SECURITY DEFINER trigger discipline, the consistent use of Supabase's server-side client for server components and browser client for client components, the explicit separation of the service_role key from public-facing code — these are the patterns the contemporary literature names as best practice. This chapter exists to reinforce what is already right, name the failure modes Servyou should keep watching for, and capture a few specific patterns worth applying as Phase 9 and Phase 10 work proceeds.

The fundamental architectural promise of Servyou's stack is that the database is the source of truth and the database enforces access rules regardless of how a query arrives. When you enable RLS on a table, PostgreSQL evaluates your defined policies before returning any results. If a user runs SELECT * FROM projects, they do not get every project in the database. They get only the projects where the policy evaluates to true for their identity. This is the property that lets a solo founder ship a multi-user marketplace with confidence; the failure modes that bring down equivalent platforms — forgotten application-level checks, refactored authorization bypasses, direct API endpoints that skip the auth middleware — are structurally impossible if RLS is enforced everywhere it needs to be.

The single most dangerous failure mode in Servyou's stack is the service_role key crossing the server-client boundary. Variables prefixed with NEXT_PUBLIC_ are exposed to the browser. Your admin keys (whether service_role or secret) must NEVER EVER have this prefix. Both formats bypass Row Level Security and provide full database access. A single import of the service-role client into a `'use client'` component would expose the key to every visitor's browser, where it could be extracted and used to bypass every security guarantee in the platform. The discipline that prevents this is the `import 'server-only'` directive at the top of the file that creates the service-role client, which causes the Next.js build to fail loudly if the file is ever imported into a client component. This should already be the case in Servyou; if it isn't, it is a critical pre-launch fix.

The case study that should sit on every Supabase founder's wall: In January 2025, over 170 applications built with AI tools were found to have fully exposed databases because developers didn't enable RLS. The pattern was the same in every case: AI-generated schemas, no explicit ALTER TABLE ENABLE ROW LEVEL SECURITY statement, the developer assuming Supabase enforces access by default. Servyou has avoided this trap because the architecture document and the data model document both enshrine "RLS on every table" as a non-negotiable. The discipline to maintain: every new table created via a migration must enable RLS in the same migration, and every Phase 9 admin operation must verify that the RLS posture has not been bypassed by the new code.

The performance implications of RLS deserve attention as Servyou's data scale grows. Every authorization system has an impact on performance. While row level security is powerful, the performance impact is important to keep in mind. This is especially true for queries that scan every row in a table. The mitigations are well-understood: index every column referenced in an RLS policy (which Servyou's schema largely does already through foreign-key indexes), add explicit WHERE clauses to queries even when RLS would filter the same rows (the explicit filter lets the query planner use indexes more aggressively), and use EXISTS subqueries rather than IN clauses where possible (EXISTS short-circuits on the first match). Phase 10 polish should include a pass over the slowest queries identified in Supabase's query insights, with index additions where they help.

The boundary between server components and client components in Next.js App Router is the second axis of safety. The pattern Servyou has converged on — data fetching in server components, mutations through client components calling server actions or direct client-side Supabase writes, RLS enforcing the rules regardless — is the right one. The failure mode to watch for is the inverse: server components that try to do client-side things (browser-only APIs like `window` or `document`), or client components that try to bypass the server flow by importing server-only utilities. Both fail loudly at build time when the boundary is respected, and the discipline of having `import 'server-only'` and `import 'client-only'` on the right files maintains the boundary.

For Phase 9 admin work specifically, the question of whether admin operations should use the service_role key needs a careful answer. The case *for* using service_role: it bypasses RLS, which means admin operations can read or modify any row without writing special admin-aware policies on every table. The case *against*: it removes the database-level safety net entirely, so any bug in admin code can affect any row, and the admin code must be unusually careful about authorization. The recommended answer is *neither* — admin operations should run with the authenticated session of the admin user, and the RLS policies on every table should grant access to users where `is_admin()` returns true. This way, the database continues to enforce the rules, and admin actions are bounded by what the admin's own user identity is allowed to do. The `is_admin()` SECURITY DEFINER function that already exists in Servyou's data model is exactly this pattern.

One specific safety pattern worth applying to Phase 9 admin operations: SECURITY DEFINER functions for any operation that needs to bypass RLS legitimately. Use SECURITY DEFINER functions for operations requiring elevated privileges. A function that runs with the function-owner's privileges rather than the calling user's privileges can do things the calling user could not, but it must include explicit authorization checks at the top of the function body. The discipline: every SECURITY DEFINER function in Servyou's schema must start with an `IF NOT is_admin() THEN RAISE EXCEPTION` guard if its purpose is admin-only. The `check_order_status_transition` and `check_job_response_limits` triggers Servyou already has are SECURITY DEFINER and include the right guards; the pattern carries forward to any new admin functions.

The auth session itself deserves a note. It's safe to trust getUser() because it sends a request to the Supabase Auth server every time to revalidate the Auth token. The implication is that `supabase.auth.getUser()` is the safe primitive for verifying the current user's identity in server components and server actions; `supabase.auth.getSession()` is faster but returns the session from the cookie without revalidating, so it can return a session that has been invalidated server-side but the client has not yet noticed. For any operation that depends on the user actually being who they claim to be (admin operations, order state transitions, payment-related actions when those arrive), `getUser()` is the right call. For pure UI personalization where stale identity is acceptable, `getSession()` is fine.

The realtime subscription primitive in Supabase deserves a careful look for Servyou's roadmap. Realtime subscriptions respect RLS policies. Users only receive updates for rows they can access. This means realtime is safe to use without writing additional authorization code, which is unusual and valuable. The use cases where realtime is worth the complexity for Servyou: the buyer's "My Requests" page showing live order status changes from the seller, the admin dashboard showing live updates to the reports queue, the freelancer's "Job Responses" page showing live notifications when a client interacts with their proposal. The use cases where realtime is *not* worth the complexity: aggregate count displays (covered in chapter eight), low-frequency administrative changes, anything where a five-second polling interval would be indistinguishable from realtime to the user.

## 8. Real-time dashboard counts without mistakes

Moatez's specific question was about how a user sees the latest numbers in their dashboard without mistakes. This is a deceptively deep topic because the right answer depends on what "latest" means in context, how expensive the count is to compute, how many users will be looking at the count, and how bad it is if the count is briefly wrong.

The naive approach — run `SELECT count(*) FROM orders WHERE seller_id = $1` every time the dashboard renders — works fine when the orders table has hundreds of rows and a single seller. It scales poorly when the orders table has hundreds of thousands of rows or when the dashboard is being rendered hundreds of times per minute. The pattern that matters is *how the count is computed* and *how often it is refreshed*.

The four strategies Servyou should know about are: live counts, cached counts with TTL, materialized views refreshed on schedule, and incrementally maintained aggregates.

Live counts are the simplest. The query runs every time the page renders; the result is always up to date. For a seller's dashboard showing today's order count, this is fine — there will not be hundreds of thousands of orders per seller, the query is fast with the right index, and the user sees real numbers. Most of Servyou's dashboard counts should be live for the foreseeable future. The pattern in Next.js: server component, `await supabase.from('orders').select('*', { count: 'exact', head: true })`, render the count. No realtime subscription, no caching, no complexity.

Cached counts with TTL are the next step when live counts get slow. Next.js supports this directly via the `revalidate` export from a page: `export const revalidate = 60` means the page is cached for sixty seconds, and the count query runs only once per minute regardless of how many visitors hit the page. By exporting a revalidate variable from our component, we can specify how many seconds we consider this data to be "fresh". For Servyou's admin overview page (where the counts of total users, total shops, total freelancers, total orders are shown), a sixty-second TTL is honest — the admin does not need to see a count that updated thirty seconds ago, and the platform avoids running the same expensive count query for every refresh.

Materialized views are the strategy for counts that are expensive to compute and changes slowly. PostgreSQL materialized views are pre-computed query results that are stored on disk and refreshed on schedule. For Servyou's eventual aggregate statistics page (counts by category, top sellers, weekly transaction volume), a materialized view refreshed once per hour or once per day is the right pattern. The query runs once at refresh time, the dashboard reads from the materialized view (which is essentially free), and the data is at most one hour stale. This is the strategy for Phase 10's statistics view, not Phase 9's admin overview.

Incrementally maintained aggregates are the most complex strategy and rarely worth the cost at Servyou's scale. The idea is to maintain a separate counters table that is updated by triggers on every insert/delete to the source table, so the count is always accurate without ever running a `count(*)` query. For Servyou, this is worth considering only for one specific case: the units-sold-per-product count that already shows on the seller dashboard. Per `product.md`, this is currently calculated live from orders, which is fine at MVP scale; if it ever becomes slow, an incrementally maintained `products.units_sold` column updated by triggers on the orders table is the canonical solution. But the optimization should wait until there is observable slowness, not be preemptive.

The strategy that does NOT belong in Servyou's MVP is realtime subscriptions for aggregate counts. Server-Side Subscriptions: Can Supabase real-time subscriptions work on the server side to trigger data updates or revalidation in a server component setup? The honest answer from the Supabase community is that realtime subscriptions are designed for the row-level changes that a user cares about in their own session (their messages, their order status changes), not for aggregate counts. Trying to use realtime for "show me the live count of all orders" is a misapplication that creates connection-count problems and doesn't actually improve the user's experience because the user wasn't looking at the dashboard the moment the count changed anyway.

For Servyou's Phase 9 admin overview specifically, the recommended pattern is: server component page with `revalidate = 60`, eight parallel count queries (total users, total shops, total freelancers, total products, total services, total job posts, completed orders, pending reports), rendered as a grid of cards at the top of the page, with a small "updated X seconds ago" timestamp showing the cache age. The simplicity of this beats any cleverer approach at MVP scale.

For a seller's "my orders today" count, the pattern is: server component page, fetch the orders directly with a WHERE filter on date range and seller_id, render. No caching needed at this scale; if the seller has 200 orders today, the query is fast.

For the buyer's "My Requests" page showing live order status, the pattern is: server component fetches the initial list, then a client component layered on top subscribes to realtime updates for that specific buyer's orders. This is the only place where realtime is worth the complexity, because the buyer is actively waiting for their order status to change and the live update genuinely improves the experience.

The discipline that prevents count mistakes is the same discipline that prevents other data mistakes in Servyou's architecture: the database is the source of truth, the application reads from it directly, and stale data is acknowledged through visible cache-age indicators rather than hidden. A user who sees "updated 30 seconds ago" on a dashboard understands that the number may have changed in the last thirty seconds. A user who sees a number with no timestamp assumes it is live, and feels betrayed when it turns out to be stale. The cache-age indicator is the trust signal that turns the stale number from a bug into an honest acknowledgment.

A specific pattern worth applying to all Phase 9 admin views: the page title or header should include the cache-refresh timestamp visibly. Something like "*Vue d'ensemble — mis à jour il y a 23 secondes*" reads as honest and professional rather than evasive about the staleness.

## 9. Admin tooling for the solo beginning developer

This chapter is the most pragmatic of the document. Moatez is one developer, learning to code as the platform gets built, with no operational team. The tooling that works for a thirty-person engineering organization is not the tooling that works for him; the tooling that works for him is what this chapter names.

The single most important admin tool today, before Phase 9 ships, is the Supabase Studio (Supabase's web dashboard). The Studio's Table Editor runs queries as service_role, which bypasses RLS, which means Moatez can see and edit every row in every table without writing any application code. This is the operational baseline; for the first ninety days post-launch, when something needs to be inspected or fixed urgently, the Supabase Studio is faster than writing custom admin UI. The Studio also includes the Auth section (every user, their session state, their metadata), the Logs section (query logs, auth events), the SQL editor (any read-only investigation), and the Storage section (uploaded files if Servyou starts using Storage). All of this is included in the Supabase free tier.

The Studio's limitations are real, however, and they are what motivates the Phase 9 admin dashboard work. The Supabase dashboard Table Editor runs queries as service_role, which bypasses RLS. This is useful for verifying data exists when a client query returns empty results — if you see data in the Table Editor but not from your app, your RLS policies are blocking the request. The Studio cannot suspend a user, cannot resolve a report with a note that surfaces to the reporter, cannot handle a dispute with structured evidence assembly, cannot enforce the audit log discipline that real moderation requires. These are the operations Phase 9 builds out.

The Phase 9 admin dashboard, when it ships, should be designed for the solo-operator reality. The pattern that has emerged from the marketplace admin-panel research is consistent: Moderation queue: Pending listing approvals, flagged content, and open disputes surfaced as a single action queue, not buried in separate module menus. Keeping operational metrics separate from strategic metrics prevents admins from drowning in trend data when they need to process a queue.

What this means for Servyou's admin dashboard: when Moatez logs in, he should see the *action queue* first, not the aggregate statistics. The action queue is the list of things that need a human decision right now — open reports, pending disputes, suspended users awaiting reactivation review, flagged content. The aggregate statistics (total users, total transactions, growth metrics) go on a separate "Statistics" page that Moatez visits weekly, not the main admin home.

The action queue itself should be ordered by triage priority, not by submission date. A first-in, first-out moderation queue treats a spam comment with the same urgency as a credible threat of violence. Effective moderation systems triage content by harm potential — ensuring the highest-risk content reaches reviewers first. For Servyou, the triage hierarchy should be: severe abuse reports (fake_scam category, harm to a specific user) → other reports → disputed orders → suspended users → flagged content. Each tier gets a distinct visual treatment so Moatez can see at a glance whether there is something urgent to handle.

Each action in the queue, when clicked, opens a detail view that contains everything Moatez needs to make a decision without leaving the page. For a report: the reported content (the actual product / service / shop / freelancer page), the reporter's profile (with their report history — repeat reporters need to be calibrated separately), the reason given, the description, any other reports about the same content. For a dispute: the order's full lifecycle history (every status transition with timestamp), the cancellation reason if any, the `received_at` timestamp if the buyer confirmed, the messages exchanged on WhatsApp if accessible. The detail view's job is to make the right decision obvious within thirty seconds of opening it.

Every admin action should be logged. Every action taken in the panel must be traceable to a named user. ... Every admin action must be timestamped and attributed to a named user, including the entity affected and the before/after state. For Servyou's solo-admin reality this matters less for accountability (Moatez is the only admin) and more for memory: when a user emails six months from now asking why their shop was hidden, the audit log is what lets Moatez reconstruct what happened. The audit log table is a simple structure: `admin_audit_log(id, admin_id, action, target_type, target_id, before_state jsonb, after_state jsonb, note text, created_at)`. Every Phase 9 mutation should write a row to this table.

The error monitoring stack for a solo developer in 2026 has converged on a few strong options. Sentry remains the dominant choice, with a free tier that gives 5,000 errors per month — which is enough for the first few months of Servyou's traffic if Servyou doesn't have a serious bug shipping. Sentry's free tier gives you 5,000 errors per month, which is generous for a solo developer. You also get basic performance monitoring, release tracking, and session replay to watch how users interact with your app before an error occurs. The case for Sentry: the smartest error grouping, the best Next.js integration, the session replay feature that lets Moatez watch what a user did before hitting an error. The case against: the pricing wall hits hard at scale (overages at roughly $0.00029 per error means one bad deploy can produce a surprise bill).

The alternatives worth knowing about: Better Stack (free tier covers multiple uptime monitors and basic log management, Sentry-SDK-compatible for error tracking with 5,000 errors/month free, modern UI, bundles uptime monitoring and incident management in one tool), Bugsink (self-hosted in a single Docker container, Sentry-SDK-compatible, free if self-hosted, the right call for founders who want to never pay for error tracking but are willing to run their own infrastructure), and GlitchTip (also self-hostable, also Sentry-SDK-compatible, open source). For Servyou specifically, the recommendation is to start with Sentry on the free tier in Phase 10, because the friction of self-hosting in addition to running the application itself is real for a solo founder. If and when the Sentry pricing becomes a problem, Bugsink is the natural migration path because the SDK is the same.

Uptime monitoring is the second piece of the monitoring stack and is often forgotten. The need: knowing when servyou.tn is down without waiting for users to email about it. Better Stack's free tier includes uptime monitoring with multiple monitors, status pages, and SMS/email alerts. UptimeRobot is the long-established free alternative (fifty monitors free at five-minute intervals, email alerts). For Servyou, either works; Better Stack's bundled error monitoring would let Moatez consolidate to one vendor if he prefers.

Database monitoring is the third piece. Supabase's built-in Database tab in the Studio shows query performance, slow queries, and connection counts. This is sufficient at MVP scale; Phase 10 polish should include a one-time review of the slowest queries with index additions where appropriate, but ongoing database monitoring beyond the Studio is not yet justified.

The deployment pipeline is the fourth piece. Vercel's built-in deployment dashboard shows every preview deploy and production deploy, with logs and rollback. Servyou's current discipline of "Vercel preview green gates the merge" is the right pattern; Phase 10 should consider upgrading from Vercel Hobby to Vercel Pro (currently around $20/month) for the additional production-grade features (per-team deployments, longer log retention, more generous build minutes). The upgrade is justified once the platform has paying real users; not before.

The combined monitoring stack for Servyou Phase 10 looks like this: Sentry (free tier) for application errors and basic performance; Better Stack or UptimeRobot (free tier) for uptime monitoring with SMS alerts; Supabase Studio for database monitoring; Vercel for deployment pipeline visibility; Cloudflare Turnstile (free) on signup and login for bot protection. Total monthly cost: zero. The cost as the platform grows: small (Sentry's first paid tier is $26/month, which is the realistic next-step expense). The cost at scale: still small relative to the platform's revenue, assuming the platform is generating revenue at that point.

The escalation discipline for a solo founder matters more than the tooling. The pattern that has emerged from the platform-incident-response literature is consistent: incidents are tiered by severity (P0 = platform-wide outage, P1 = critical flow broken, P2 = cosmetic), the response is matched to the tier (P0 = immediate attention regardless of time of day, P1 = same-day fix, P2 = scheduled in next session), and the post-mortem after every P0 or P1 is non-negotiable. For Servyou's solo-founder reality, the post-mortem is a one-paragraph note in `docs/incidents/<YYYY-MM-DD>-<summary>.md` capturing what happened, what was fixed, what would prevent recurrence. This documentation is what turns single incidents into accumulated wisdom; without it, the same incident happens three times.

A specific pattern that helps solo developers: the "deployment journal." Every production deploy gets a one-line note in a journal (a simple markdown file or even a private Slack channel) saying what was shipped and the deploy hash. When something breaks at 2am, the journal is what lets the founder identify which deploy might have caused it without having to scroll GitHub history with bleary eyes. For Servyou, this can be as simple as a `docs/deployment-journal.md` file appended to after every merge to main.

## 10. The recommended four-week pre-launch sequence

The chapters above have surveyed the strategic and engineering landscape. This final chapter ties them together into a concrete sequence: what to do in the four weeks between now (Phase 9 admin dashboard work in progress) and a real launch to Tunisian users. This is opinionated; it can be adjusted as conditions change, but the sequence reflects the priorities the previous chapters established.

Week one is dedicated to Phase 9 completion. The admin dashboard arc (PR-I1 through PR-I6) needs to ship so the moderation infrastructure exists before real users start using the platform. The chapters above named the specific shape the admin dashboard should take: action queue first, statistics page separate, every action logged to an audit table, severity-triaged report queue with detail views that surface decision context. By the end of week one, the admin can suspend users, resolve reports, hide content, and handle disputes from a single interface. The platform is operationally ready.

Week two is dedicated to Phase 10's safety net. Three pieces: Sentry integration for error monitoring (one focused PR with the Next.js SDK and the appropriate scrubbing of sensitive fields before transmission), Cloudflare Turnstile integration on signup and login (one focused PR with the widget and the server-side token validation), and the backup-and-restore runbook (a documented procedure for capturing a Supabase backup and restoring it in case of catastrophe, including the test of actually doing it once end-to-end). By the end of week two, the platform has the standard safety net that real users deserve.

Week three is dedicated to the standalone pages, the public surfaces, and the legal review. The Terms of Service, Privacy Policy, Cookie Policy, and Accessibility Statement are drafted from templates appropriate to Tunisian regulation, reviewed by a qualified Tunisian lawyer in a one-hour consultation, revised based on the lawyer's feedback, and published. The About, Contact, and FAQ pages are written in Moatez's voice and built. The 404 and 500 pages are styled to match the platform. The footer is implemented properly on every page. The homepage is polished — even before the full design system arrives, the hero section gets its first deliberate pass with the headline, subheadline, primary CTA, three benefits, and trust indicators arranged according to the patterns in chapter two. By the end of week three, the platform looks publicly presentable.

Week four is dedicated to the soft launch and the first user interviews. Five to ten thirty-minute conversations with prospective Tunisian consumers, shop owners, and freelancers — recruited through Moatez's existing network or through the Tunisian tech community — using the platform as it stands, with Moatez taking notes on confusion, friction, and suggestions. These interviews are not formal user research; they are the cold-start phase of the marketplace literature, the founder doing things that do not scale. The insights from these interviews inform the final polish before the public launch. By the end of week four, the platform is ready to be opened to real users at low marketing volume.

The fifth week — outside the four-week pre-launch window — is the real launch. The supply side (the first fifty shop owners and freelancers recruited through Moatez's network) is in place. The demand side starts arriving through the first wave of marketing — Moatez's own social channels, the Tunisian tech communities he is part of, the sellers' existing followings. The first paid users emerge, the first transactions complete, and the build-measure-learn loop activates.

What is deliberately NOT in this four-week sequence: the full design system, the full brand identity work, the dropshipping connector for Converty, the in-platform payment integration, the buyer reputation system, the Arabic translations of the new keys accumulated since PR-A. These are deliberately deferred because they do not block launch. The launching version of Servyou is French-only, has the existing visual style (not yet the polished design system), has no paid acquisition spend, has no in-platform payment, and serves only the Tunis-Ariana-Ben Arous cluster. This is enough to learn from real users. Everything else gets sequenced after the launch based on what real users teach Servyou about what they need.
